Science / Light and the body clock

Your body uses light to tell time.

Light does more than illuminate a room. To understand its role after dark, look at timing, intensity, duration and spectrum together.

Color and spectrum

Warm-looking light can have different spectra

Color describes appearance. A spectrum describes the distribution of light across wavelengths. Those are different questions—and neither, by itself, tells us how one person will sleep.

How the retina signals the body clock

A signal to the body clock

Retinal cells containing melanopsin help relay light information involved in daily timing. Rods and cones also contribute. This non-visual pathway is why a light’s effect cannot be described by appearance alone.

CIE S 026:2018 ↗

Color temperature alone cannot explain
light’s biological effects

Correlated color temperature describes the appearance of near-white light. It does not uniquely specify its spectrum. Melanopic EDI weights light by melanopsin sensitivity and expresses it relative to daylight—not as a guaranteed biological outcome.

Lume is a saturated orange-red source. Ordinary white-light CCT comparisons alone cannot establish its performance.

Esposito & Houser, 2022 ↗

Light exposure across the day

Daytime
Bright light at appropriate times helps support the body’s daily rhythms.
Evening
Reduce light exposure as bedtime approaches.
Nighttime
Keep your sleep environment as dark as possible. When light is needed, consider its intensity, spectrum, and duration.

General guidance for healthy adults. Brown et al., 2022 ↗

How to evaluate Lume’s design and effects

Lume applies a research-informed design intention to nighttime light. To evaluate the product itself, spectral measurements need defined conditions at the eye; biological and sleep claims need suitable human studies. One cannot substitute for the other.

Sleep Lab’s self-reported experiences answer a different question: how the product fits real routines. They do not establish melatonin levels or clinical sleep outcomes.
